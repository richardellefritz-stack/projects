# Record Progressions and the Midday Spectacle

Two documents, far apart in time.

One is a federation book that lists successive athletic marks. The other is a letter warning Lucilius off crowds. They are primary evidence for a narrower claim: the IAAF publishes official record progressions, and Seneca reports a midday spectacle whose outcome was death.

## The IAAF progression book

The IAAF publishes official world-record progression lists. The 2015 edition, edited by Richard Hymans and Imre Matrahazi, is the eighth edition of that book. It tabulates successive marks by event.

The title page names the 2015 edition and the two editors. The Introduction (p. VII) calls this the eighth edition of the IAAF World Records Progression book. Page 3 lists the events for which World Records are recognised, under IAAF RULE 261. Page 5 opens the men’s tables with 100 yards: successive dated marks from 1855.

That is the document. It is a published list of successive marks, event by event.

## The midday show

Seneca writes to Lucilius to warn him off crowds. He says he happened into a midday spectacle (*meridianum spectaculum*) expecting relief from human blood. He found instead “mera homicidia”: men with no armor. The outcome of the fighters is death. The crowd cries “Occide, verbera, ure.” When the show paused: “interim iugulentur homines, ne nihil agatur.”

Seneca is moralizing against the crowd. The letter is a warning to Lucilius, not a neutral report, and not a Kyle typology of executions, venationes, and gladiators. Any use of this scene as evidence of lethal spectacle must carry that concession in the open.

## What the distance is

People sometimes treat a published record table and a midday spectacle as the same kind of public contest. That reading leans on the audience. The documents are a different matter.

The IAAF book publishes successive marks by event under a named rule. Seneca’s letter records a midday program of unarmored men whose outcome is death, with the crowd calling for more killing when the program pauses.

The claim is limited to those two features: an official progression of marks, and a midday show Seneca reports as *mera homicidia*. The letter does not supply a typology. The book does not supply a philosophy of records.
