# A Room Is an Argument

Grant that a room can be beautiful. People notice light, proportion, a surface that holds the eye.

A pretty room that cannot gather anyone, teach anyone, or keep anyone is decoration. It has won the glance and lost the day. Aesthetics without a task is a mood you can hang on a wall. Architecture begins when the room has a job: a place to meet, to hear, to wait, to be judged, to be left alone together. Ask what the room is for.

I have walked students into empty lecture halls and asked them what the room wanted from them. They start with how it looks. The seats face one way because someone already decided who speaks and who listens. The door sits where it sits for the same reason. Praise the finish and you have read the room as a picture.

We start with a room doing work, and we judge that work before we collect the postcard.

## A Building Is a Made Object

If a room is an argument, a building is a made object. Someone chose the span, the finish, and what would stand through weather and through a crowd. After you judge the beauty, the thing still has to stand.

Vitruvius, *De Architectura* 1.3.2, requires that building take account of *firmitas*, *utilitas*, and *venustas*: strength, utility, and beauty. Strength comes from foundations carried to solid ground and from materials chosen without stinginess. Utility comes from an arrangement that does not impede use. Beauty comes from a pleasing appearance and due proportion. Neither a feeling nor a slogan is on that page. The page is a made-object rule.

You can love how a place looks and still ask who made it stand, and for whom. Tuesday asks both, looks first.
