# Chapter 2: Why You're Underpriced (and It's Not Your Fault)

Underpricing is not usually one mistake. It's three or four independent mistakes that all happen to push in the same direction, made by someone with no information at a moment when they had very little leverage.

That's worth sitting with for a second, because the most common reaction to the Chapter 1 diagnostic is a kind of low-grade shame — *how did I not notice this* — and shame is a bad starting condition for a negotiation. If you go into Chapter 6's conversation feeling like you've been getting away with something, it will show. So before we fix the number, let's be precise about how it got broken. You'll notice that almost none of it was a failure of intelligence.

## Mistake one: you anchored on the wrong market

When you were setting your first rate, you almost certainly looked around to see what other people charged. That's a reasonable instinct. The problem is where you looked.

If you looked at freelance marketplaces — the big bidding platforms — you were sampling from the most price-compressed corner of the entire market. Aggregated data on this is fairly consistent: platform rates commonly run something like **20–40% below what comparable freelancers charge working directly with clients**. Same skills, same output, dramatically different number, because platforms put a large number of providers in direct visible competition on price and give clients an easy way to sort by cost.

So a freelancer who benchmarks against platform listings and then prices slightly under them — the natural move when you're new and want to win — has anchored to a discounted market and then discounted further.

And it gets worse, because the range within any category is enormous. Depending on the source, the same broad job title can span a factor of five or ten. Virtual assistant work, for example, runs from roughly $4–$15/hour in some offshore markets to multiples of that for specialized US-based work. "Content writer" covers people charging $0.06/word and people charging many times that. When ranges are that wide, "what other people charge" is not a number. It's a distribution, and you get to choose where in it you stand — but only if you know it's a distribution.

**None of this was visible to you at the time.** You were looking at real numbers that real people were really charging. They were just the wrong real numbers.

## Mistake two: you priced from take-home, not from cost

Here's the arithmetic error, and it's close to universal.

When most people set a freelance rate, they reason from the salary they want. *I'd like to make $70,000. That's about $35 an hour at full time. I'll charge $40 and that's a bit of a cushion.*

Every step of that is wrong, and we'll fix it properly in Chapter 3. For now, the two things it misses:

**Taxes and expenses come out first, and they're bigger than you think.** As a freelancer you cover the full self-employment tax burden — both halves of what an employer would otherwise split with you — which is a meaningful chunk off the top before income tax even enters the picture. Then business expenses: software, hardware, insurance, professional development, the health coverage an employer used to subsidize. It is entirely normal for a freelancer's **effective rate — what actually reaches you per hour worked — to run something like 35–45% below their billed rate.** If you priced against your desired salary, you priced against a number you will never see.

**A billable hour and a working hour are not the same thing.** Which is mistake three, and it deserves its own section.

## Mistake three: you assumed a 40-hour billable week

This one costs more than any other single error, and it's the one people defend hardest.

You do not bill 40 hours a week. You will never bill 40 hours a week. Nobody does, and the people who claim to are counting wrong.

Consider what a normal week actually contains. Client calls that aren't billed. Writing proposals for work you may not get. Invoicing and chasing invoices. Bookkeeping. Answering emails. Marketing yourself so there's work next month. Fixing your own equipment. Learning the thing you'll need to know next quarter. None of that is on a client's invoice, and all of it is work.

When you look at aggregated data on how independent professionals actually spend their time, the realistic number lands somewhere around **20–30 billable hours per week** for most people. And the year is shorter than you think, too: subtract vacation, holidays, and the weeks where you're sick or something goes wrong, and you're realistically looking at **48–50 working weeks**, not 52.

Run those two corrections together and the damage is severe. If you set your rate assuming 40 billable hours across 52 weeks, you built your entire financial model on roughly 2,080 billable hours. The realistic figure might be closer to 1,200. You have overestimated your annual capacity by something in the neighborhood of 40%, and your rate is proportionally too low to hit the income you were aiming for.

This is the single biggest reason freelancers work constantly and still feel broke. The rate was never wrong for the hours they imagined. It was wrong for the hours that exist.

## Mistake four: the fear

The three mistakes above are informational. This one isn't, and it's the reason the other three persist after you know about them.

Industry analyses and practitioner accounts converge on the same short list of reasons freelancers won't name a higher number, and it's remarkably consistent across fields:

**Fear of losing the client.** The dominant one, by a distance. The imagined downside of naming a higher rate — they say no, you look greedy, you lose the income — is vivid and immediate. The downside of naming a lower one is abstract and deferred. Humans are not good at weighing those symmetrically.

**Comparison to "real" experts.** You look at people further along in your field, conclude you're not that yet, and price to match your self-assessment. The trouble is that this is self-confirming: charging like a beginner is one of the more reliable ways to keep being treated like one, which then confirms the original assessment.

**Price as a proxy for quality.** This cuts the opposite way from how anxiety assumes. Clients cannot easily evaluate the quality of professional work before they buy it, so they use available signals — and price is a loud one. A rate far below the market range doesn't read as "good value." It reads as "there's something wrong with this one." Underpricing can actively cost you the clients you'd most want.

**The absence of a script.** This is the practical barrier hiding inside the emotional one, and it's often the whole problem. Many freelancers who fully accept that they're underpriced still don't raise rates, because they cannot picture the specific sentence they'd say to a specific client on a specific Tuesday. Chapter 6 is entirely about supplying that sentence.

## The trap: all four push the same way

Here's why the total damage exceeds the sum of the parts.

If these mistakes were random, some would push your rate up and some down, and they'd partially cancel. They don't. Anchoring low, pricing from take-home, overestimating billable hours, and flinching from the conversation **all push in the same direction.** They stack.

Which is why, when you look at how far the typical freelancer sits below a sustainable rate, the figure that keeps surfacing across sources is somewhere in the range of **30–50%** — not a rounding error, but not a mystery either once you see that four independent forces were all pressing on the same side of the scale.

## One thing to take out of this chapter

You did not fail at business. You made a series of individually reasonable decisions with incomplete information, under conditions specifically designed to compress your price, and then a normal human aversion to uncomfortable conversations kept those decisions in place.

That's a fixable situation. The information problem gets solved in the next chapter, which is just arithmetic. The conversation problem gets solved in Chapter 6, which is just a script.

The shame isn't load-bearing. You can put it down.
