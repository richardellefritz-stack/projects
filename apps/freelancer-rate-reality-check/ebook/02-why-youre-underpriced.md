# Chapter 2: Why You're Underpriced (and It's Not Entirely Your Fault)

There's a version of this chapter that would tell you the problem is confidence, and that you should believe in yourself more. That version would be useless.

The real answer is more mechanical and more forgivable: **you set your first rate under conditions that guaranteed it would be too low, and then a set of ordinary human tendencies kept it there.** Understanding the mechanism matters, because you can't dismantle something you think is just a personal failing.

## The Conditions That Produced Your First Rate

Rewind to the moment you picked your first number. You almost certainly had:

- **No pipeline.** One prospect, maybe. Losing them meant losing everything.
- **No portfolio to point at.** Nothing that let you say "this is what I'm worth" with evidence.
- **No idea what the work actually costs you.** You hadn't yet discovered how many unbillable hours a client relationship generates.
- **A reference set drawn entirely from the cheapest visible market.** This is the big one.

That last point deserves unpacking, because it's the single most consequential thing that happens to a new freelancer's pricing.

## You Benchmarked Against the Cheapest Visible Market

When you were figuring out what to charge, where did you look? Almost everyone looks at the same places: freelance marketplaces, job boards, public rate threads. Those are the visible surfaces of the freelance economy — which is exactly the problem, because **the visible surfaces are systematically the cheapest part of it.**

Here's what the aggregated data suggests: rates on the large freelance platforms run roughly **20–40% below** direct-hire contractor rates for comparable work and comparable talent. A freelancer billing $80/hour through a marketplace and one billing $120/hour direct may be the same caliber of professional doing the same caliber of work. The platform isn't measuring quality; it's compressing price through visible competition. When every bid is displayed next to every other bid, the pressure runs in exactly one direction.

So when you set your rate by scanning the marketplace and positioning yourself "competitively" — meaning slightly below what you saw — you didn't calibrate to your market. You calibrated to the discount floor of your market, and then discounted yourself below that.

There's a second layer. Those visible rates skew even lower than they appear, because the freelancers most likely to be publicly bidding at low rates are the ones with the least leverage: the newest, the least specialized, the ones in the lowest-cost geographies. Global rate spreads are enormous — US rates commonly run several multiples of rates in lower-cost regions, and within a single category like virtual assistance the spread between markets can be five to ten times. If your comparison set silently included people operating in a completely different cost environment, your "competitive" number was never competitive with anything relevant to you.

## Then Anchoring Locked It In

Once a number exists, it becomes hard to move. Industry analyses of pricing behavior frequently describe an **anchoring** dynamic: the first figure introduced into a negotiation exerts disproportionate influence over everything that follows. In freelancing, you're the one who introduced it — and then you have to keep negotiating against your own past self.

This operates on two fronts simultaneously, which is why it's so sticky.

**With clients, the anchor is your history.** Your existing client knows what you charge. Any increase is measured against that known baseline, not against the market. Going from $50 to $80 is a 60% increase to them, regardless of the fact that $80 is entirely ordinary for the work. The bigger the gap between your anchor and your real rate, the more impossible the correction looks — so you don't attempt it, and the gap grows.

**With yourself, the anchor becomes self-concept.** This one is quieter and does more damage. After enough time quoting $50, that number starts to feel like a description of your worth rather than a decision you made once under bad conditions. Quoting $90 to a new prospect feels like a lie you're telling. It isn't — but the feeling is real, and it's the feeling that governs what comes out of your mouth on a discovery call.

## The Fear of Losing the Client Is Miscalibrated

Strip away the mechanics and there's a single fear doing most of the work: **if I charge more, they'll say no.**

It's the most consistently reported barrier to raising rates across essentially every practitioner account. And it's not irrational — sometimes they will say no. But the fear is badly calibrated in three specific ways.

**It treats every lost deal as pure loss.** It isn't. A prospect who walks because your rate went from $60 to $85 was, by definition, a client who valued the engagement at under $85/hour. Losing them frees capacity for a client who doesn't. If your rate increase costs you a third of your clients but raises your rate 40%, you're working substantially less for similar or better money — with the least price-tolerant portion of your book removed. That math is not intuitive in the moment, which is why it needs saying explicitly.

**It assumes clients are shopping primarily on price.** Some are. But practitioners consistently report that established clients weigh things price-shopping ignores: not having to re-explain the project to someone new, not gambling on an unknown, not absorbing the delay of a search. There is real switching cost baked into your relationship, and it's usually worth more than the increase you're afraid to ask for.

**It ignores what a low price signals.** Where buyers can't easily evaluate quality in advance — which is most professional services — price becomes a proxy for it. This is well-worn territory in pricing practice: unusually low prices don't read as "good value," they read as "something is wrong here." Being the cheapest option can actively cost you the clients you most want, before you ever get to demonstrate the work.

## The Comparison Trap Confirms Itself

One more contributor, and then we'll get to arithmetic.

Many freelancers underprice because they don't feel like the "real experts" they're comparing themselves to. There's always someone more established, more visible, more credentialed, and it feels presumptuous to charge near them.

The trap is that this belief confirms itself. You feel less established, so you charge less, so you attract clients who buy on price, so your work is less prestigious and your case studies weaker, so you feel less established. The loop closes. Nothing about the loop is driven by your actual skill level — it's driven entirely by the price you set at the start.

This is also why "raise your rates once you're more experienced" is bad advice in practice. It sounds prudent, but it never triggers, because the feeling of not-yet-being-ready doesn't resolve on a schedule. The people who break the loop generally do it the other way around: they raise the price first, and the positioning follows.

## The Fix Is Mechanical, Not Personal

None of the above is a character flaw. You anchored on a market that was structurally cheaper than yours, at a moment when you had no leverage, using a method nobody told you was broken. Then ordinary anchoring behavior kept the number where it was.

That's a mechanical problem, and mechanical problems have mechanical fixes. The next chapter is the fix: a number built from your actual costs and your actual available hours, that you can show your work on.

Once you have that, the confidence question mostly takes care of itself. It's considerably easier to say a number out loud when you can explain exactly where it came from.
