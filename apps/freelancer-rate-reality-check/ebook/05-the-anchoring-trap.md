# Chapter 5: The Anchoring Trap

You have a defensible number. Now comes the part where most people stall.

The gap between knowing your rate and charging it is not an information gap. You already have all the information you need. It's a gap created by the number you set first — which has quietly become the reference point that everything else gets measured against, including by you.

## Why the First Number Sticks

Anchoring is one of the most reliably observed patterns in how people evaluate prices: **the first figure introduced becomes the reference point for every judgment that follows.** It doesn't have to be a good number, or a justified one. It just has to be first.

In most negotiations, the anchor comes from the other side. In freelancing, you set it yourself — which means you spend the rest of your career negotiating against a number invented by the least experienced version of you, under the worst conditions you'll ever have (Chapter 2).

It operates on two fronts.

**With clients, the anchor is your history with them.** Your client at $55/hour doesn't evaluate $85 against the market. They evaluate it against $55. To them it's not "a normal rate for this work" — it's a 55% increase, and it feels like one. The market context you have is invisible to them.

**With yourself, the anchor is your self-concept.** This one is more limiting. After a few years at $55, that number stops feeling like a decision and starts feeling like a description of what you are. Saying $110 to a new prospect feels like overreaching, even when you have a spreadsheet proving it's correct. The number in your head, not the number in your spreadsheet, is what comes out of your mouth on the call.

The practical consequence: **the longer you hold a low anchor, the more impossible correcting it looks** — so you don't try, so it holds longer. The trap tightens on its own.

## Underpricing Compounds; Overpricing Self-Corrects

Here's the structural unfairness at the center of this.

Being overpriced is a recoverable error. Quote too high, lose a deal, adjust. The market corrects you quickly and cheaply, and you've lost one prospect.

Being underpriced is a compounding one. Nothing tells you it's happening — the feedback is *positive*. Clients say yes. Your pipeline fills. Every signal reads as success while you lose tens of thousands of dollars a year, silently, for years. And unlike the overpricing error, it doesn't self-correct: it entrenches, because each additional month at the low rate deepens the anchor with both your clients and yourself.

This asymmetry is why the bias in your pricing decisions should run *upward*. The downside of asking too much is a lost deal. The downside of asking too little is a lost decade.

## New Prospects and Existing Clients Need Opposite Approaches

The critical thing to understand: **you don't have one pricing problem. You have two, and they need different handling.**

**New prospects have no anchor.** They've never heard a number from you. Whatever you quote *is* the anchor. This is free money, and most freelancers leave it on the table by quoting their historical rate out of habit rather than their calculated one.

**Existing clients have a strong anchor.** They know your number, and any change is measured against it. This requires actual management — covered in Chapters 6 and 7.

The strategic implication is that these get handled separately and on different timelines:

> **Quote your new rate to every new prospect starting immediately.** Not after you've built more confidence, not after your next case study. Immediately, on the next inquiry. There's no relationship to damage and no anchor to overcome. The only thing standing between you and the higher rate is saying it.
>
> **Migrate existing clients deliberately, over time.** With notice, in structured increments, using the strategy for your level in Chapter 6 and the scripts in Chapter 7.

This is the single highest-leverage move in this book, and it costs you nothing. Most of the fear about raising rates is fear about existing relationships. That fear has essentially no bearing on what you quote a stranger next week — and yet it governs that quote anyway, purely by habit.

## Increment or Correct? Match the Move to the Gap

For existing clients, there's a real strategic question: incremental increases, or one correction to your calculated rate?

The conventional advice favors **incremental**, and it has genuine merits: smaller increases are easier for clients to absorb, less likely to trigger a budget review, and let you discover the ceiling of what a given client tolerates without betting the relationship on one conversation.

But incrementalism has a cost that rarely gets named: **if your gap is 40–50%, annual 10% increases take four to five years to close it** — during which you're still underpriced, still losing the difference every year, and still reinforcing the anchor with each cycle. Incrementalism is a good tool for maintenance and a poor tool for correction.

A reasonable way to decide:

- **Gap under ~20%?** Increment. One increase closes it, easily absorbed.
- **Gap of 20–40%?** Two steps over six to twelve months, announced together so the client sees the whole path up front.
- **Gap over ~40%?** Recognize you're not raising a rate, you're correcting a structural error. Segment your clients: keep the ones worth keeping on a defined migration path, and let the bottom of the book go while you replace it at the new rate with new prospects who have no anchor.

That last case feels dramatic. It's usually less dramatic in practice than it sounds, because clients at the bottom of a badly-underpriced book are typically also the most demanding relative to what they pay. Replacing them is frequently a quality-of-life improvement independent of the money.

How aggressively you can execute any of this depends on leverage you may or may not have yet — which is exactly what the next chapter sorts out.

## What Actually Happens When You Raise Rates

The fear says: they'll all leave.

What practitioners consistently report is closer to this: **most clients accept a reasonable, well-communicated increase, and the ones who leave are disproportionately the ones you least wanted.**

That pattern makes sense structurally. Clients who chose you primarily on price will leave over price — that was always the basis of the relationship. Clients who chose you for judgment, reliability, or knowing their business have real switching costs: onboarding someone new, re-explaining context, absorbing the risk that the replacement is worse. That switching cost is usually worth considerably more to them than the increase you're nervous about asking for.

And if some do leave? Run the arithmetic before assuming that's a loss. Lose 25% of your clients and raise your rate 40%, and you're at roughly **105% of prior revenue for 75% of the work.** You didn't lose. You got a raise and your evenings back.

That calculation is the antidote to the fear, because the fear is fundamentally an arithmetic error — it treats client count as the thing to maximize, when the thing to maximize is income per hour of your life.

## Three Things to Do Before Chapter 6

Do this now, while it's concrete:

1. List your current clients with their rates.
2. Mark each one: **keep and migrate**, or **let go over time**.
3. Write down the date of your next new-prospect conversation — that's when the calculated rate goes into effect, with no exceptions.

Step 3 requires nothing from anyone but you. Do that one even if you do nothing else in this book.
