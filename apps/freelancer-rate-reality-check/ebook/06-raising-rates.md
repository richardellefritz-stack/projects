# Chapter 6: Raising Rates Without Losing the Client

This is the chapter you'll actually use. Everything before it was preparation.

What follows are the specific moves and the specific language. Adapt the wording to sound like you — but keep the structure, because the structure is doing most of the work.

## Five Rules That Govern Every Version

**1. State it. Don't ask permission.**
The most common failure is phrasing an increase as a request: *"Would it be okay if I raised my rate?"* That invites a negotiation you didn't need to have and signals you don't believe the number. Rates are business decisions you announce, the way every business you buy from announces theirs. Your accountant doesn't ask your permission.

**2. Give notice.**
Thirty to sixty days is standard and reads as professional. A rate change effective immediately reads as a demand; the same change effective in six weeks reads as a policy.

**3. Don't over-explain.**
The instinct is to justify at length — costs, inflation, experience, a paragraph of reasons. Long justifications read as apology, and every additional reason is an additional surface to argue with. One sentence of context is plenty. Confidence is conveyed by brevity.

**4. Never apologize.**
No "I'm so sorry to do this," no "I hate to be the bearer of bad news." You are not doing something to them. You are pricing your work correctly. Apology invites pushback by signaling that you think the increase is unreasonable.

**5. Decide your floor before the conversation.**
Know in advance the lowest number you'll accept and what you'll do if they decline. Deciding this in the moment, under pressure, is how people end up back where they started.

## Script 1: The Standard Increase (Ongoing Client)

For a good client, a reasonable gap, a relationship you want to keep.

> **Subject: Rate update, effective [date ~45 days out]**
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I wanted to give you advance notice that my rate is moving to **$[X]/hour**, effective **[date]**. Everything invoiced before then stays at the current rate.
>
> I've really valued working on [specific project/thing], and I'm looking forward to continuing. Happy to talk through anything.
>
> [You]

That's the whole thing. Note what's absent: no apology, no lengthy justification, no request for approval, no anxious hedging. Notice, new number, continuity, done.

The most common edit people make to this template is adding a paragraph explaining themselves. Resist it.

## Script 2: The Grandfathered Increase

For long-tenured clients where you want to preserve the relationship above all — or where the gap is large enough that a full correction would be a shock.

> Hi [Name],
>
> A heads-up that my standard rate is moving to **$[X]/hour** for new work starting **[date]**.
>
> Because we've worked together since [year], I'm holding your rate at **$[Y]/hour** through **[date ~6 months out]**, after which it'll move to the standard rate.
>
> Wanted you to have plenty of runway. Let me know if you'd like to talk it through.

This works well for two reasons: it makes the increase concrete and scheduled rather than vague and threatening, and it explicitly frames the current rate as a courtesy — which reinforces that the higher number is the real one, not an aspiration.

One caution: grandfathering has a shelf life. If you grandfather indefinitely, you've simply kept the old anchor with extra steps. Always attach a date.

## Script 3: The Scope Reset

When a relationship has quietly expanded — more meetings, more revisions, more "quick questions" — without the rate ever changing. Extremely common, and the increase is easier to make here because it's obviously fair.

> Hi [Name],
>
> Looking back over the last few months, the scope of what I'm handling has grown quite a bit beyond our original agreement — [specific examples: the weekly calls, the additional review rounds, the ad-hoc requests].
>
> I'd like to reset this properly. Two options:
>
> **A.** Keep the current scope at **$[X]/hour**, reflecting the actual work involved.
> **B.** Return to the original scope at the current rate, with anything beyond it quoted separately.
>
> Either works for me — which would you prefer?

The two-option structure is doing deliberate work: it shifts the conversation from *whether* to *which*, and it makes the increase feel like a fairness correction rather than an ask — because it is one.

## Script 4: The New Prospect

The easiest one, and the one people fumble most often — because there's no script, just a number said without flinching.

> "My rate for this kind of work is **$[X]/hour**."
> 
> Or, for project work: "For the scope we've discussed, this would be **$[X]**."

Then **stop talking.**

This is the hard part. Silence after a number feels unbearable, and the instinct is to fill it — usually by softening ("but I have some flexibility"), justifying, or worst of all, negotiating against yourself before they've said a word. Every one of those undoes the number.

Say it. Stop. Let them respond. A pause is not rejection; it's someone thinking.

## Handling the Four Common Responses

**"That's more than we budgeted."**
> "I understand. I can work within a $[their number] budget by adjusting scope — [specific reduction]. Would that work?"

Reduce scope, not rate. This is the most important negotiating habit in the book. Dropping your rate teaches the client your number is soft and permanently resets your anchor with them. Adjusting deliverables keeps the rate intact and makes the trade-off explicit.

**"Can you do better on price?"**
> "That's my rate for this scope. If budget is the constraint, I'm glad to look at what we could adjust to fit it."

Same move. Notice you haven't said no — you've said the price corresponds to the work, which is both true and non-confrontational.

**"[Competitor] charges less."**
> "That's likely true — there's a wide range in this market. What I bring is [specific: knows your systems, no ramp-up time, handles X end-to-end]. If price is the deciding factor, they may be the better fit."

Acknowledge it, differentiate, and be genuinely willing to lose the deal. A client shopping purely on price will churn on price eventually anyway; better to find out now than after you've discounted.

**"We'll have to think about it."**
> "Of course. I'll follow up [specific day] if I haven't heard from you."

Then actually follow up on that day. Don't fill the silence with a discount. A lot of "we'll think about it" turns into yes when you don't panic.

## When They Say No

Some will. Plan for it before it happens.

If an existing client declines the increase, you have three moves: hold firm and let them go, offer a defined grandfather period as a landing ramp (Script 2), or reduce scope to fit their budget at your new rate. What you should *not* do is quietly revert. Reverting doesn't just cost you this increase — it teaches that client that your rates move when they push, which makes every future increase harder.

And run the arithmetic before treating a loss as a loss. From Chapter 5: losing a quarter of your clients while raising your rate 40% leaves you at roughly the same revenue for three-quarters of the work. Losing your two lowest-paying, highest-maintenance clients is frequently the best thing that happens to a freelance business in a given year.

## The Recurring Version

One structural change that prevents all of this from being necessary again.

Most freelancers raise rates reactively — when they're desperate, resentful, or fully booked. That's why it feels like a confrontation: it's always overdue and emotionally loaded by the time it happens.

The alternative is to make it a schedule rather than an event:

- **Recalculate your rate every six months** (Chapter 3's five steps take ten minutes once you've done them once). Costs change, tax situations change, and per Chapter 4, your market may be moving underneath you in either direction.
- **Raise rates for existing clients on an annual cycle**, at a consistent time of year, communicated as ordinary business practice.
- **Reprice new prospects continuously** — every new quote reflects the current calculation, not last year's.

An annual increase that clients expect is a non-event. A 45% correction after four years of silence is a crisis. Same money, entirely different conversation. Choose the schedule.
