# Chapter 1: The Reality Check

Let's find out where you stand before we do any math.

Answer these honestly. Not aspirationally — honestly, based on the last six to twelve months of your actual working life.

---

**1. When was the last time a prospective client said your rate was too high?**

If the answer is "never" or "I can't remember," mark that.

**2. What percentage of the proposals or quotes you send turn into work?**

If it's above roughly two-thirds, mark that.

**3. Has your rate changed since you started freelancing?**

If it's the same number you picked in your first month, or it's moved less than the cost of living has, mark that.

**4. How did you arrive at your current rate?**

If the honest answer involves the phrase "it felt about right," or "it's what everyone else on the platform was charging," or "that's what the first client offered," mark that.

**5. Do you know your effective hourly rate — what you actually clear per hour worked, after tax, expenses, and all the hours you spend on work you don't bill for?**

If you don't know that number, mark that.

**6. When you calculate how much you'll earn this year, do you multiply your rate by 40 hours a week?**

If yes — or if you've never actually done this calculation at all — mark that.

---

## Scoring this honestly

There's no clever point system. Here's the blunt version:

**If you marked four or more:** You are very likely underpricing, and probably by a wide margin. Not slightly. The rest of this book is going to be uncomfortable and then useful.

**If you marked two or three:** You're probably underpriced in a way you could fix in an afternoon of arithmetic and one difficult conversation.

**If you marked zero or one:** You've either done this work already or you're in an unusual position. Read Chapter 3 anyway to check your math, then skip ahead to Chapter 7.

## The one that surprises people

Most freelancers reading that list expect the damning question to be number four — the one about picking a number that felt right. It's embarrassing, but it's also fixable with a calculator, and everyone knows they did it.

The question that actually catches people off guard is number two: your close rate.

Here's the counterintuitive part. If you win nearly every job you quote on, your instinct is to read that as good news. You're persuasive. Clients like you. Your pipeline is healthy.

But think about what a very high close rate actually tells you. It means essentially nobody is walking away from your price. Which means you have never found the ceiling — you have no evidence about what the market would have tolerated, because you've never asked for enough to find out. A rate low enough that literally everyone accepts it is, by definition, a rate you could have raised.

This pattern shows up repeatedly in practitioner accounts of pricing: consultants and agency advisors who work with a lot of independent professionals tend to converge on the same rough heuristic, which is that a close rate comfortably above two-thirds is a signal of underpricing rather than of sales skill. The specific threshold people cite varies, and you shouldn't treat any exact percentage as gospel. The underlying logic is what matters: **losing some deals on price is evidence that your price is doing work. Losing none means it isn't.**

This is genuinely hard to internalize, because losing a deal feels bad and winning one feels good, and the feedback is immediate in both cases. The cost of underpricing, meanwhile, is invisible and spread across every invoice you send. You never get a notification that says "you just left $2,400 on the table." You just quietly don't get the money.

## What underpricing actually costs

It's tempting to think of underpricing as a per-project shortfall. You charged $2,000 for something worth $3,000, so you lost $1,000. Annoying, but bounded.

That framing badly understates it, for three reasons.

**It compounds across every hour you work.** A rate is not a one-time decision, it's a multiplier applied to your entire year. If you're 30% underpriced — which, as we'll see in Chapter 2, is well within the normal range — you are 30% underpriced on every hour of every project for as long as that rate stands.

**It sticks.** This is the part that turns a mistake into a trap, and it's the whole subject of Chapter 5. The rate you set early becomes the reference point that both you and your clients measure all future rates against. Doubling your rate is not a change from $50 to $100; it is a change from "the number everyone has agreed I'm worth" to "a number I now have to justify." The longer you sit at a low rate, the more expensive the correction becomes — not in money, but in nerve.

**It selects your clients for you.** A low rate doesn't just attract more clients, it attracts a *particular kind* of client: the ones optimizing for price. Those clients tend to be more demanding about scope, slower to pay, and quicker to leave for someone cheaper — which is a recurring theme in practitioner accounts, and it makes intuitive sense. Someone who chose you because you were the cheapest option will leave when a cheaper option appears. So the low rate doesn't just reduce your income; it fills your calendar with the clients least likely to ever accept a higher one, which makes escaping harder.

That third effect is the one that turns underpricing from a math error into a structural problem. You end up with a book of business that is, in a sense, selected against your ability to fix your rates.

## What this book is going to do about it

In Chapter 3, you'll calculate an actual number — a floor below which the work is not worth doing, and a target above it. The arithmetic is simple. It's genuinely just arithmetic, and it takes about ten minutes.

> **Run your own numbers.** Chapter 3 walks through the math by hand so you understand it. If you'd rather have it done for you, the **Rate Reality Check calculator** takes the same inputs and returns your floor, your target, and how both compare to the going range for your category and experience level. Do it either way — but do it before you read Chapter 6, because the back half of this book assumes you have a number.

Then Chapter 4 puts that number in context against what your market actually pays, because a rate that's sustainable for you but wildly out of step with your field is a different problem that needs a different fix.

And then — the part most pricing advice skips — Chapters 5 and 6 deal with the fact that you already knew you were underpriced before you picked up this book, and knowing it hasn't been enough.

Because here's the thing about the diagnostic you just took: most people who score badly on it were not surprised. Some part of you already suspected. The problem was never information. It was that raising your rate means an uncomfortable conversation with someone whose good opinion you need, and no amount of arithmetic makes that conversation happen by itself.

So we'll do the math first. Then we'll deal with the actual obstacle.
