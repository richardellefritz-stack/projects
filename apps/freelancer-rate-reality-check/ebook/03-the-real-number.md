# Chapter 3: The Real Number

This is the arithmetic chapter, the one most people skip before wondering why nothing changed.

We're going to build your rate from the ground up, in five steps. The companion calculator at https://calculate-my-freelance-rate.netlify.app/ does all of this automatically, but read the steps anyway, because the *reason* each one exists is what you'll need when a client asks you to justify your price.

## The Method That Halves Your Rate

First, the method almost everyone starts with:

> "I want to make $80,000. There are 2,080 working hours in a year. So $80,000 ÷ 2,080 = **$38/hour**."

Every part of this is wrong in the same direction. It assumes you can bill every working hour, which you can't. It assumes you work every week of the year, which you don't. It ignores self-employment tax, which is not optional, and business expenses, which are real. And it treats $80,000 as revenue when you meant it as take-home.

Stack those five errors and the method routinely produces a number around **half** of what's actually required. This is the single largest mechanical cause of underpricing, and it is completely fixable.

> **About the worked example that follows.** The figures below, a $70,000 target with $9,000 expenses, 30% tax, and 25 billable hours across 48 weeks, produce a $94 floor and a $113 recommended rate. They are one illustration of the method, not a typical or expected result. Change any input and every downstream number changes. Run your own figures rather than adopting these.

## Step 1: Start With What You Want to Keep

Write down your target **take-home** income, meaning what lands in your account after taxes, the amount you'd actually live on.

Be honest rather than modest. If a comparable salaried role in your field pays $85,000, don't write $55,000 because freelancing feels like it should cost you something. Freelancing has real advantages, but it also means you're absorbing costs an employer used to cover, which is precisely what the next steps account for.

> **Worked example:** Target take-home = **$70,000**

## Step 2: Add Your Business Expenses

Every dollar you spend to operate has to come out of revenue before it becomes income. Add up the annual cost of:

- Software and subscriptions (design tools, project management, hosting, AI tools)
- Hardware, amortized. A $3,000 laptop replaced every three years is $1,000/year.
- Health insurance, if you're buying it yourself. For many US freelancers this is the largest single line item and the most overlooked. At $500–800/month, that's roughly $6,000–$9,600/year of revenue existing solely to cover coverage.
- Professional insurance, if applicable
- Internet, phone, workspace or coworking
- Accounting and legal
- Marketing, website, portfolio hosting
- Professional development, courses, conferences

Most solo freelancers land somewhere in the **$5,000–$15,000/year** range, though this varies enormously by field. A photographer with equipment is not in the same universe as a copywriter with a laptop, so use your real numbers.

> **Worked example:** Expenses = **$9,000**
> Running subtotal: $70,000 + $9,000 = **$79,000**

## Step 3: Gross Up for Tax, Dividing Rather Than Multiplying

Here is where the biggest single miss usually happens.

As a freelancer you pay **both halves** of Social Security and Medicare, the employee portion and the employer portion your old job used to quietly cover. That's 15.3% combined, applied to roughly 92.35% of net earnings, so the effective bite is around 14%. Half of it is deductible against income tax, which softens the blow somewhat. All of that comes *before* federal and state income tax.

All in, most US freelancers should plan for total tax obligations in the **25–40%** range depending on income level, state, and deductions. Where you land inside that band is a question for an accountant rather than a book, but you need a working figure now, and you need to apply it correctly.

The correct operation is **division, not multiplication.** This trips people up constantly:

- Wrong: $79,000 × 1.30 = $102,700
- Right: $79,000 ÷ (1 − 0.30) = **$112,857**

The first version leaves you short, because you'd owe tax on the grossed-up amount too. Use division every time.

> **Worked example:** assume 30% total tax.
> $79,000 ÷ 0.70 = **$112,857 required annual revenue**

## Step 4: Find Your Real Billable Hours, Which Are Not 2,080

Now the other half of the equation, and the other place the standard method collapses.

**Weeks per year.** There are 52, and you will not work 52. Subtract two weeks of vacation, one to two weeks of holidays and sick days, and you land at **48–50 working weeks**. Use 48 if you'd like to take real time off, which you should.

**Billable hours per week.** This is the number people get most wrong. A 40-hour week is not 40 billable hours; it never is. The rest goes to non-billable work: finding clients, writing proposals, invoicing and chasing payment, email, calls that aren't scoped, admin, taxes, portfolio and marketing, and learning.

Realistic billable capacity for most solo freelancers is **20–30 hours per week** out of a 40-hour working week. Time-tracking data broadly supports this. Roughly a third of tracked professional time tends to be non-billable, and solo operators generally fare worse, because no one else absorbs the overhead. If you believe you're an exception, track a normal week honestly before assuming it.

> **Worked example:** 25 billable hours × 48 weeks = **1,200 billable hours/year**

Note what just happened. Not 2,080, but **1,200**. That gap alone, before tax and before expenses, accounts for a large share of typical underpricing.

## Step 5: Divide, Then Add Margin

> **Required revenue ÷ billable hours = your minimum viable rate**
>
> $112,857 ÷ 1,200 = **$94/hour**

Compare that to the broken method's $38/hour for the same person wanting a similar life. That is not a rounding difference; it is a different business.

### Floor vs. recommended

The $94 figure is your **floor**, the rate at which you exactly hit your target with zero margin for error. It assumes you fill every one of those 1,200 hours, that nothing goes wrong, that no client pays late, and that you never have a slow quarter.

You want a buffer above it. Add **15–25%** for slow periods, scope creep, bad-debt risk, and reinvestment:

> $94 × 1.20 = **$113/hour recommended**

That's your number. Not a guess, but a calculation you can walk anyone through line by line.

## Billable Rate vs. Effective Rate

One more concept, and it reframes everything.

Your **billable rate** is what you charge. Your **effective rate** is what you actually net per hour of your working life, after tax, after expenses, and spread across every hour you work rather than only the ones you invoice. Those are dramatically different numbers, and our example freelancer has two versions worth comparing.

**At the floor rate of $94/hour**, they bill $112,857, keep $79,000 after tax, and net exactly their $70,000 target after expenses. Spread across a working year of roughly 1,920 hours, that comes to about **$36/hour of actual life**, against a $94 sticker price.

**At the recommended $113/hour**, they bill about $135,600 and net roughly **$85,920** after tax and expenses. That comes to about **$45/hour of actual life**, against a $113 sticker price. The extra $19/hour of billing is what turns a zero-margin year into one that survives a slow quarter.

Either way, notice the size of the gap. **The amount that reaches you is well under half the number you quote.** Not 10% less, not 30% less, but under half.

Sit with that, because it accomplishes two useful purposes. First, it explains the busy-but-broke feeling from Chapter 1 with precision. You weren't imagining the gap between your rate and your bank balance; the gap is structural, and it's larger than almost anyone estimates.

Second, and more usefully, **it makes your rate feel defensible.** The number that felt outrageous to say out loud stops feeling outrageous once you can see that well over half of it never reaches you. You're not charging $113 because you're worth triple a salaried employee. You're charging $113 because that's what it costs to net a normal income while carrying every expense an employer used to carry for you. That isn't greed; it's arithmetic.

When a client pushes back, this is the frame to use. Not "I'm very experienced," which is an opinion they can dispute, but rather: this is what independent work costs. That's a fact they can't argue with.

## Run Your Own Number Before Continuing

Do it before Chapter 4, in five steps:

1. Target take-home
2. Plus annual expenses
3. Divided by (1 − tax rate)
4. Divided by (billable hours/week × working weeks), giving your floor
5. Times 1.15–1.25, giving your recommended rate

Then put the result next to the number you wrote down at the end of Chapter 1. https://calculate-my-freelance-rate.netlify.app/ The gap between those two figures, multiplied by your annual billable hours, is what this has been costing you per year.
