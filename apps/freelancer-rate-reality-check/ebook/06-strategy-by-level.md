# Chapter 6: What Leverage You Actually Have

You have a number, and you know why it's hard to say. What you don't have yet is a strategy, and the right strategy is not the same for everyone.

The arithmetic in Chapter 3 doesn't change with experience. A beginner and a fifteen-year specialist calculate their floor identically. What changes is **leverage**: how fast you can move, how much resistance you can absorb, and what you can afford to lose. Get this wrong in either direction and it costs you. Beginners who attempt an aggressive correction they can't survive lose clients they needed, while experts who creep upward in timid 8% steps leave years of income on the table for no reason at all.

## The Four Factors That Determine Leverage

Before the levels, know what you're actually measuring.

1. **Pipeline depth.** Can you afford to lose this deal? This is the dominant factor, and everything else is secondary to whether losing the negotiation ends your month.
2. **Evidence.** Can you point to results, or only to effort? Evidence converts a rate from a claim into a conclusion.
3. **Specialization.** How many people could plausibly replace you? Chapter 4 covered why this is the strongest multiplier available.
4. **Switching cost.** How embedded are you? Someone who knows a client's systems, history, and preferences is expensive to replace regardless of tenure.

Experience bands are a proxy for these four, and an imperfect one. A two-year specialist with a full pipeline has more leverage than a seven-year generalist with two clients and no waiting list. **Read the four factors, then use the band that matches, not the one your calendar says.**

## Beginner (0–2 Years): Climb Until Someone Pushes Back

**Your leverage is low, and pretending otherwise is expensive.** Thin pipeline, limited evidence, high replaceability. The strategy has to respect that.

**Don't** attempt a large correction with existing clients. You can't absorb the churn, and a failed increase at this stage does lasting damage to your nerve.

**Do** run the ladder. Quote your Chapter 3 floor to the next new prospect, then raise your quote **10–15% every second or third new prospect**, and keep climbing until someone pushes back.

That pushback is the entire point. It is the only way to find the edge of your market, and no other method will produce the information. Most freelancers never find the edge because they stop climbing long before they reach it, then spend years assuming the ceiling sits much lower than it does.

Three rules while you're climbing:

- **Never quote below your floor.** Below the floor you are paying for the privilege of working. Turn it down or cut scope.
- **Expect to lose some.** Losing roughly one prospect in four is a healthy signal rather than a failure. Losing none means you're still below the edge.
- **Take 50% upfront** on anything longer than a week. At this level, one client paying ninety days late is a genuine crisis. Payment terms are leverage you have even when rate leverage is thin.

What you are building in this phase is not income. It is evidence and pipeline, the two assets that make the next phase possible.

## Intermediate (2–5 Years): Segment Your Book and Prune It

**This is where durable underpricing lives.** Enough clients to be busy, not enough structure to be profitable. If you recognized yourself in Chapter 1's busy-but-broke description, you're probably here.

Your leverage is real but uneven, strong with some clients and weak with others, so stop treating your book as a single population. **Sort every client on two axes: what they pay, and how much friction they generate.** Then:

- **C-tier (low rate, high friction):** raise aggressively, to full standard rate, with 30 days' notice. You can afford to lose them and you should want to. These are the clients whose departure creates capacity for everything else.
- **B-tier (fair rate, ordinary friction):** raise to standard rate on a normal cycle.
- **A-tier (good rate or strategically valuable):** grandfather briefly, with a firm date. Never indefinitely.

Two structural moves matter more than the rate increase itself at this level.

**Set a minimum engagement size.** Small jobs carry the same admin, onboarding, invoicing, and context-switching overhead as large ones, spread across far fewer billable hours. A $400 minimum project fee frequently improves effective rate more than a 15% rate increase does, because it eliminates the work that was quietly destroying your utilization.

**Start quoting projects instead of hours** wherever scope is definable. Project pricing tends to run 20–40% above equivalent hourly billing (Chapter 8), and it stops penalizing you for getting faster — the structural flaw in hourly you'll eventually have to solve anyway.

**Tactic: the two-option quote.** When budget is uncertain, present the full engagement at your rate and a genuinely smaller engagement at a lower total. Never the same scope at two prices. The conversation shifts from *whether* to *which*, and both options protect your rate.

## Advanced (5–10 Years): Sell Access, Not Hours

**Your leverage is strong.** You're specialized, you have evidence, and replacing you costs a client real money and real time. Price accordingly, and change what you're selling.

Hourly should now be your least-used instrument. Move toward retainers for ongoing access and priority, day rates for concentrated work, and project fees for defined outcomes.

**Build the increase into the contract from the start.** A single line, *"Rates are reviewed annually and adjusted with 30 days' notice,"* converts every future increase from a negotiation into an administrative event. This is the highest-return sentence in your contract, and almost nobody includes it.

**Use terms as levers.** Deposits, net-15 payment, defined revision counts, kill fees on cancelled projects, and explicit out-of-scope pricing all protect effective rate without touching the headline number. Clients negotiate them far less aggressively than price.

**Start declining work.** Declining is a pricing action. It raises your effective rate by freeing capacity for better-paid engagements, and it changes how prospects read your availability. If you have never turned down a project, you are not yet pricing at this level regardless of your tenure.

## Expert (10+ Years): Use Price as a Filter

**At this level price is not a target. It's a filter.** The operating question changes from what they will pay to who you want to work with, and your rate is the primary instrument for answering it.

**Quote the number at which you would be genuinely content to lose the deal.** Not the number you hope they accept, but the number where a no costs you nothing you wanted. If you are not regularly losing engagements at that price, the number is too low. That's uncomfortable, and it's correct.

**Sequence the conversation.** Have the fit-and-outcome discussion *before* the price discussion, always. When a prospect has articulated what the result is worth to them, your number arrives as a conclusion rather than a request. Leading with price at this level wastes your strongest asset.

**Waitlists are legitimate** when they're true, and they do more for perceived value than any amount of positioning copy.

**If you discount, discount downward in the market**, to a nonprofit, an early-stage founder, or a cause you care about. Never to your best-funded client. A discount granted to someone who could easily pay full rate is not generosity; it's a rate cut with extra steps.

## What to Trade Instead of Price

This applies at every level, and it may be the single most useful section in the chapter.

When budget genuinely binds, you have currencies other than your rate. **Concede any of these before you concede a dollar:**

| Instead of cutting price, trade… | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Scope** | Fewer deliverables, fewer revision rounds, narrower brief |
| **Timeline** | Off-peak scheduling, longer runway, flexible deadline |
| **Payment terms** | Full payment upfront in exchange for a small reduction, the only discount genuinely worth giving |
| **Commitment** | A modest reduction for a six-month retainer instead of month-to-month |
| **Rights** | Case study, named testimonial, portfolio use, reference calls |
| **Referrals** | A written introduction to two comparable buyers |

The principle underneath: **a concession given without receiving anything teaches the client your number is soft.** A concession traded for something teaches them it's real. Same dollar amount, opposite lesson.

## Scope Creep Is a Rate Cut in Instalments

No one negotiates your rate down as effectively as you do, one small favor at a time. The fix is procedural rather than confrontational.

- **Write the scope, including a "not included" list.** The exclusions do more work than the inclusions.
- **Keep one sentence ready** for out-of-scope requests: *"Happy to take that on. It's outside our current scope, so I'll send a quick add-on quote."* Said warmly, immediately, every time.
- **Never do the first free favor silently.** Do it if you like, but name it: *"I'll fold this one in. Normally this would be a separate line item."* The first unnamed favor sets the pattern for every one after it.

## The Five Rules That Hold at Every Level

1. **Anchor first** wherever you can. Whoever names a number first shapes the range.
2. **Never lower the rate without removing something.** Price and scope move together or not at all.
3. **Decide your walk-away number before the conversation**, not during it.
4. **Silence after your number is not rejection.** It's someone thinking. Let it sit.
5. **Never negotiate against yourself.** If you've named a number and they haven't responded, the next word is theirs.
