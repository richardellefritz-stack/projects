# Chapter 9: Your Next 30 Days

Reading this changes nothing. What follows is the part that does.

The plan below is deliberately small. Most people who read a pricing book do nothing — not because they disagree, but because "raise my rates" is a vague intention with no first move. So here's the first move, and the ones after it.

## Week 1: Get Your Number

**Day 1 — Run the calculation.** Chapter 3's five steps, using your real figures. Ten minutes with the calculator, twenty by hand. `[CALC]`

- Target take-home
- Plus annual business expenses
- Divided by (1 − tax rate)
- Divided by (billable hours/week × working weeks) → your floor
- Times 1.15–1.25 → your recommended rate

**Day 2 — Check it against the market.** Chapter 4. Where does your number sit against your category and band? If it's below, use the band. If it's above, check your inputs, then consider whether the answer is positioning rather than price.

**Day 3 — Write it down and commit.** One number. This is your rate now — not a goal, not something to grow into. Put it where you'll see it before your next client conversation.

**Day 4 — Calculate the cost of waiting.** Take the gap between your old rate and your new one, multiply by your annual billable hours. That figure is what each year of delay costs you. Keep it next to the rate.

## Week 2: Sort Your Clients and Pick Your Strategy

**Day 8 — Identify your leverage level.** Chapter 6. Pipeline depth, evidence, specialization, switching cost. Be honest — the strategy that works at Intermediate will hurt you at Beginner, and the one that protects a Beginner leaves an Expert badly underpriced.

**Day 9 — List and sort every client.** Rate, annual value, and how much friction they generate. Then group them:

- **Migrate:** worth keeping. Rate increase on a schedule.
- **Grandfather:** long-tenured or strategically valuable. Defined grace period, firm end date.
- **Release:** low-paying and high-maintenance. No action needed — stop replacing them at the old rate and let attrition work.

**Day 10 — Pick your dates and your script.** Which client gets the conversation first, when, and which script from Chapter 7. Start with the one you're least anxious about — get a win before the hard one.

## Week 3: Switch Your New-Prospect Rate

This is the highest-leverage week, and it requires nothing from anyone but you.

**Day 15 — Update everything public.** Website, proposal templates, rate sheet, profiles. New number everywhere.

**Day 16 — Rehearse the sentence.** Out loud. *"My rate for this kind of work is $[X]/hour."* Then silence. Say it until it stops feeling like a lie — usually about a dozen repetitions. This sounds absurd and it works, because the difficulty was never the number. It was hearing yourself say it.

**Day 17 onward — Quote the new rate to every new inquiry.** No exceptions, no "just this once because they seem price-sensitive." New prospects have no anchor with you (Chapter 5). Whatever you say first becomes the reference point. Say the right number.

## Week 4: Have the First Conversation

**Day 22 — Send it.** Your first existing-client increase, using the script you chose. Notice period 30–45 days out.

Send it in the morning, then close the email tab and work on something else. The waiting is worse than the outcome, essentially always.

**Day 25 — Handle the response.** Most will simply accept. If pushback comes, use the responses in Chapter 7 — and remember the core rule: **adjust scope, not rate.**

**Day 28 — Schedule the rest.** With one conversation behind you, put the remaining migrations on the calendar with real dates. They're markedly easier after the first.

**Day 30 — Set two recurring reminders.**

- **Every 6 months:** recalculate your rate. Costs move, tax situations move, and your market may be moving in either direction (Chapter 4).
- **Every 12 months:** annual increase for existing clients, same time each year, as routine business practice.

That second reminder is what prevents you from ever needing this book again.

## The Seven Things That Matter

If you remember nothing else:

1. **Your rate is a calculation, not a feeling.** Take-home, plus expenses, divided by (1 − tax), divided by real billable hours, plus margin.
2. **You have far fewer billable hours than you think.** Roughly 1,200, not 2,080. This one error accounts for most underpricing.
3. **Quote the new rate to new prospects immediately.** No anchor exists. This costs nothing and is the easiest gain available.
4. **Match your strategy to your leverage.** Ladder up if you're new; segment and prune if you're established; filter with price if you're senior.
5. **When pushed, reduce scope — never the rate.** And trade for something whenever you concede.
6. **A near-perfect close rate is a symptom, not an achievement.**
7. **Put it on a calendar** so it never becomes a crisis again.

## This Was Never About Confidence

The gap between where you are and where you should be didn't open because you're bad at your job. It opened because you set a number under bad conditions, with no method, in a market whose visible surface is its cheapest part — and then ordinary anchoring kept it there.

That's a mechanical problem. You now have the mechanical fix.

The only thing left is saying the number out loud to someone who might say no. That part doesn't get easier by waiting, by getting more experienced, or by feeling ready. It gets easier by doing it once.

Your next inquiry is the one. Quote the new number.
