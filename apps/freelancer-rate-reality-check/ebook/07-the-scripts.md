# Chapter 7: The Scripts

This is the chapter you'll actually use. Everything before it was preparation.

Adapt the wording so it sounds like you — but keep the structure, because the structure is doing most of the work. Each script is tagged with the levels it fits best.

## Five Rules Behind Every Script

**1. State it. Don't ask permission.**
The most common failure is phrasing an increase as a request: *"Would it be okay if I raised my rate?"* That invites a negotiation you didn't need to have and signals you don't believe the number. Rates are business decisions you announce, the way every business you buy from announces theirs. Your accountant doesn't ask your permission.

**2. Give notice.**
Thirty to sixty days is standard and reads as professional. A rate change effective immediately reads as a demand; the same change effective in six weeks reads as a policy.

**3. Don't over-explain.**
The instinct is to justify at length — costs, inflation, experience, a paragraph of reasons. Long justifications read as apology, and every additional reason is another surface to argue with. One sentence of context is plenty. Confidence is conveyed by brevity.

**4. Never apologize.**
No "I'm so sorry to do this," no "I hate to be the bearer of bad news." You are not doing something *to* them. You are pricing your work correctly. Apology invites pushback by signaling that you think the increase is unreasonable.

**5. Decide your floor before you open the conversation.**
Know the lowest number you'll accept and what you'll do if they decline. Deciding this under pressure, mid-conversation, is how people end up back where they started.

## Script 1 — The Standard Increase

*All levels. Your default.*

> **Subject: Rate update, effective [date ~45 days out]**
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I wanted to give you advance notice that my rate is moving to **$[X]/hour**, effective **[date]**. Everything invoiced before then stays at the current rate.
>
> I've really valued working on [specific project], and I'm looking forward to continuing. Happy to talk through anything.
>
> [You]

That's the whole thing. Note what's absent: no apology, no lengthy justification, no request for approval. Notice, new number, continuity, done.

The most common edit people make to this template is adding a paragraph explaining themselves. Resist it.

## Script 2 — The Grandfathered Increase

*Intermediate and above. For A-tier clients, or when the gap is large enough that a full correction would be a shock.*

> Hi [Name],
>
> A heads-up that my standard rate is moving to **$[X]/hour** for new work starting **[date]**.
>
> Because we've worked together since [year], I'm holding your rate at **$[Y]/hour** through **[date ~6 months out]**, after which it'll move to the standard rate.
>
> Wanted you to have plenty of runway. Let me know if you'd like to talk it through.

This works for two reasons: it makes the increase scheduled and concrete rather than vague and threatening, and it explicitly frames the current rate as a courtesy — reinforcing that the higher number is the real one.

**Always attach a date.** Grandfathering without an end date is just keeping the old anchor with extra steps.

## Script 3 — The Scope Reset

*All levels. Extremely common, and the easiest increase to make, because it's visibly fair.*

For relationships that have quietly expanded — more meetings, more revisions, more "quick questions" — without the rate ever moving.

> Hi [Name],
>
> Looking back over the last few months, the scope of what I'm handling has grown well beyond our original agreement — [specific examples: the weekly calls, the extra review rounds, the ad-hoc requests].
>
> I'd like to reset this properly. Two options:
>
> **A.** Keep the current scope at **$[X]/hour**, reflecting the actual work involved.
> **B.** Return to the original scope at the current rate, with anything beyond it quoted separately.
>
> Either works for me — which would you prefer?

The two-option structure shifts the conversation from *whether* to *which*, and frames the increase as a fairness correction — because it is one.

## Script 4 — The New-Prospect Quote

*All levels. Most important for Beginners, and the one people fumble most.*

There's no template here. Just a number, said without flinching.

> "My rate for this kind of work is **$[X]/hour**."
>
> Or, for project work: "For the scope we've discussed, this would be **$[X]**."

Then **stop talking.**

This is the hard part. Silence after a number feels unbearable, and the instinct is to fill it — by softening (*"but I have some flexibility"*), by justifying, or worst of all by negotiating against yourself before they've said a word. Every one of those undoes the number.

Say it. Stop. Let them respond. A pause is not rejection; it's someone thinking.

## Script 5 — The Three-Option Quote

*Intermediate and above. Use when budget is unknown or the buyer is comparison-shopping.*

> Hi [Name],
>
> Based on our conversation, here are three ways we could approach this:
>
> **Essential — $[X]**
> [Core deliverable only. Defined, narrow, genuinely useful.]
>
> **Standard — $[Y]**
> Everything in Essential, plus [the things most clients actually need].
>
> **Complete — $[Z]**
> Everything in Standard, plus [depth, speed, or ongoing support].
>
> Most clients in your situation choose Standard. Happy to walk through any of them.

Three things this does that a single quote can't: it moves the decision from yes/no to which-one, it lets the buyer self-select on budget without you discounting, and the top option makes the middle one feel moderate. Name the middle option as the common choice — that's where most buyers will land, so make sure it's the one you actually want to sell.

**Each tier must differ in scope, not just price.** Three prices for the same work is a discount menu, and buyers see through it immediately.

## Script 6 — The Contracted Annual Adjustment

*Advanced and Expert. The one that ends this problem permanently.*

Put this in the agreement at the start:

> **Rates.** Fees are reviewed annually and may be adjusted with 30 days' written notice.

Then, when the time comes:

> Hi [Name],
>
> Per our agreement, this is the annual rate review. Effective **[date, 30+ days out]**, the rate moves to **$[X]/hour**.
>
> Everything else stays as-is. Let me know if you have questions.

An increase you agreed to in advance isn't a negotiation — it's an administrative notice. This single clause is worth more over a career than any individual negotiation you'll ever win.

## Script 7 — Declining Without Burning the Bridge

*Advanced and Expert. Declining is a pricing action — see Chapter 6.*

> Hi [Name],
>
> Thanks for thinking of me for this. It's not the right fit for me at [$X budget / this scope], so I'll pass — but I'd genuinely like to stay in touch for future work.
>
> If it's useful, [Name] does strong work in this area and may have capacity: [contact].
>
> Best of luck with it.

Clean, warm, and final. No apology, no lengthy explanation, no leaving the door open to a lower number. The referral costs you nothing and is remembered.

## The Five Responses You'll Actually Hear

**"That's more than we budgeted."**
> "I understand. I can work within a $[their number] budget by adjusting scope — [specific reduction]. Would that work?"

Reduce scope, not rate. This is the most important negotiating habit in the book. Dropping your rate teaches the client your number is soft and permanently resets your anchor with them. Adjusting deliverables keeps the rate intact and makes the trade-off explicit.

**"Can you do better on price?"**
> "That's my rate for this scope. If budget is the constraint, I'm glad to look at what we could adjust to fit it."

Same move. Notice you haven't said no — you've said the price corresponds to the work, which is both true and non-confrontational.

**"[Competitor] charges less."**
> "That's likely true — there's a wide range in this market. What I bring is [specific: knows your systems, no ramp-up, handles X end-to-end]. If price is the deciding factor, they may be the better fit."

Acknowledge, differentiate, and be genuinely willing to lose it. A client shopping purely on price will churn on price eventually anyway.

**"Could you do a lower rate to start, and we'll raise it later?"**
> "I've found that doesn't tend to work out for either of us — the starting rate becomes the reference point. I'd rather start at $[X] with a smaller initial scope, and grow from there."

This is the single most dangerous offer you'll receive, because it sounds reasonable and it is almost never honored. Chapter 5 explains exactly why: whatever you start at becomes the anchor. Trade scope, never the rate.

**"We'll have to think about it."**
> "Of course. I'll follow up [specific day] if I haven't heard from you."

Then actually follow up on that day. Don't fill the silence with a discount. A great deal of "we'll think about it" becomes yes when you don't panic.

## When They Say No

Some will. Plan for it before it happens.

If an existing client declines an increase, you have three moves: hold firm and let them go, offer a defined grandfather period as a landing ramp (Script 2), or reduce scope to fit their budget at your new rate.

What you should **not** do is quietly revert. Reverting doesn't just cost you this increase — it teaches that client that your rates move when they push, which makes every future increase harder than this one was.

And run the arithmetic before treating a loss as a loss. From Chapter 5: lose a quarter of your clients while raising your rate 40%, and you're at roughly the same revenue for three-quarters of the work. Losing your two lowest-paying, highest-maintenance clients is frequently the best thing that happens to a freelance business in a given year.

## Make It a Schedule, Not an Event

One structural change prevents all of this from being necessary again.

Most freelancers raise rates reactively — when they're desperate, resentful, or fully booked. That's why it feels like a confrontation: by the time it happens it's overdue and emotionally loaded.

The alternative:

- **Recalculate every six months.** Chapter 3's five steps take ten minutes once you've done them once.
- **Raise existing-client rates annually**, at a consistent time of year, communicated as ordinary practice — ideally under a contract clause (Script 6).
- **Reprice new prospects continuously.** Every quote reflects the current calculation, not last year's.

An annual increase clients expect is a non-event. A 45% correction after four years of silence is a crisis. Same money, entirely different conversation.
