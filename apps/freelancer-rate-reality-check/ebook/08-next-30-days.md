# Chapter 8: Your Next 30 Days

Reading this changes nothing. What follows is the part that does.

The plan below is deliberately small. Most people who read a pricing book do nothing, not because they disagree, but because "raise my rates" is a vague intention with no first move. So here's the first move, and the four after it.

## Week 1: Get Your Number

**Day 1 — Run the calculation.** Chapter 3's five steps, using your real figures. Ten minutes with the calculator, twenty by hand. `[CALC]`

- Target take-home
- Plus annual business expenses
- Divided by (1 − your tax rate)
- Divided by (realistic billable hours/week × realistic working weeks)
- Times 1.15–1.25 for margin

**Day 2 — Check it against the market.** Chapter 4. Where does your number sit against your category and tier? If it's below the band, use the band. If it's above, check your inputs, then consider whether the answer is positioning rather than price.

**Day 3 — Write it down and commit.** One number. This is your rate now. Not a goal, not something to grow into. Put it somewhere you'll see it before your next client conversation.

**Day 4 — Calculate the cost of waiting.** Take the gap between your old rate and your new one, multiply by your annual billable hours. That figure is what each year of delay costs you. Keep it next to the rate.

## Week 2: Sort Your Clients

**Day 8 — List everything.** Every current client, their rate, roughly what they pay you annually, and an honest note on how demanding they are relative to that.

**Day 9 — Sort into three groups.**

- **Migrate:** clients worth keeping. They get a rate increase on a schedule.
- **Grandfather:** long-tenured or strategically valuable. Defined grace period, then standard rate. Always with a date.
- **Release:** low-paying and high-maintenance. No action needed — you simply stop replacing them at the old rate, and let attrition do the work.

**Day 10 — Pick your dates.** Which client gets the conversation first, and when. Start with the one you're least anxious about — you want a win before the hard one.

## Week 3: The New-Prospect Switch

This is the highest-leverage week and it requires nothing from anyone but you.

**Day 15 — Update everything public.** Website, proposal templates, rate sheet, profiles. New number everywhere.

**Day 16 — Rehearse the sentence.** Out loud. "My rate for this kind of work is $[X]/hour." Then silence. Say it until it stops feeling like a lie — usually about a dozen repetitions. This sounds absurd and it works, because the difficulty was never the number, it was hearing yourself say it.

**Day 17 onward — Quote the new rate to every new inquiry.** No exceptions, no "just this once because they seem price-sensitive." New prospects have no anchor with you (Chapter 5). Whatever you say first becomes the reference point. Say the right number.

## Week 4: The First Conversation

**Day 22 — Send it.** Your first existing-client increase, using Script 1 or 2 from Chapter 6. Notice period 30–45 days out.

Send it in the morning, then close the email tab and work on something else. The waiting is worse than the outcome, essentially always.

**Day 25 — Handle the response.** Most will simply accept. If pushback comes, use the responses in Chapter 6 — and remember the core rule: **adjust scope, not rate.**

**Day 28 — Schedule the rest.** With one conversation behind you, put the remaining migrate-group conversations on the calendar with real dates. They're markedly easier after the first.

**Day 30 — Set two recurring reminders.**

- **Every 6 months:** recalculate your rate. Costs move, tax situations move, and your market may be moving in either direction (Chapter 4).
- **Every 12 months:** annual increase for existing clients, same time each year, as routine business practice.

That second reminder is what prevents you from ever needing this book again. An expected annual increase is a non-event. A 45% correction after four years of silence is a crisis. Same money, completely different conversation.

## The Short Version

If you remember nothing else:

1. **Your rate is a calculation, not a feeling.** Take-home, plus expenses, divided by (1 − tax), divided by real billable hours, plus margin.
2. **You have far fewer billable hours than you think.** 1,200, not 2,080. This one error accounts for most underpricing.
3. **Quote the new rate to new prospects immediately.** No anchor exists. This costs you nothing and is the single easiest gain available.
4. **Migrate existing clients deliberately**, with notice, on a schedule.
5. **When pushed, reduce scope, not rate.**
6. **A near-perfect close rate is a symptom, not an achievement.**
7. **Put it on a calendar** so it never becomes a crisis again.

## One Last Thing

The gap between where you are and where you should be didn't open because you're bad at your job. It opened because you set a number under bad conditions, with no method, in a market whose visible surface is its cheapest part — and then ordinary anchoring kept it there.

That's a mechanical problem. You've now got the mechanical fix.

The only thing left is saying the number out loud to someone who might say no. That part doesn't get easier by waiting, by getting more experienced, or by feeling ready. It gets easier by doing it once.

Your next inquiry is the one. Quote the new number.
